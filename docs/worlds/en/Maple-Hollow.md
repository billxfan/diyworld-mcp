# Maple Hollow

**Genre:** Cozy social, persistent small-town life  
**Locale:** Fictional Northeastern United States  
**Player promise:** *Small actions can make a place feel like home.*

## World premise

Maple Hollow sits where the Maple River bends around an old mill town. Main Street holds a post office, a diner called The Lantern, a small clinic, and a public library in the former passenger depot. The spring flood marks are still visible on the brickwork. Freight passes through at night, though passenger service ended years ago.

East of the library, a separate freight house stands beside the old mill siding. It has been closed since flood damage three years ago. The town is not waiting for a hero to reopen it. It needs people who will show up twice, keep a promise, and notice what needs doing.

You arrive by late bus with one bag, no local address, and as much or as little personal history as you choose to share.

## What play feels like

You do not accept abstract quests. You encounter local situations: a missed shift, a broken freezer, a disputed fence, a library request, a room-to-let notice, a clinic delivery, a community meeting, or a tradition someone is trying to keep alive.

Each meaningful action should affect at least one of four things:

- Who knows you, what they remember, and whether they trust you
- What access you have to a home, job, place, service, or invitation
- What changes in the town's shared record
- What practical thread a later player can discover, continue, repair, or dispute

The point is not to become the town's savior. It is to become a person with a credible place in it.

## First scene

The rain has just stopped when you step off the late bus outside The Lantern. Ruth Calder, the mail carrier, has slipped on the wet pavement, and a canvas bag of sealed letters has spilled across the curb. Mae Alvarez is holding an umbrella over her while trying to keep the nearest envelopes out of a puddle.

Mae looks at your bag and asks:

> “You got somewhere to sleep tonight?”

You can:

- Help retrieve the sealed mail under Ruth's direction, without reading names or addresses
- Ask Mae about the handwritten room-to-let notice in The Lantern's window
- Check whether Ruth is hurt and offer to carry the bag as far as the post office

These are openings, not a closed menu. You may take any plausible action.

## First three turns

1. **Make an immediate choice.** Help with the spill, pursue a lodging lead, assist Ruth, or take another grounded action.
2. **Receive a specific consequence.** A particular person responds, a practical obstacle appears, and you earn or lose a credible lead.
3. **Leave a trace.** A delivery is completed, a shift is offered, a rental lead is recorded, or a note appears on the public board.

A refusal, missed connection, or poor first impression should change timing, trust, or access, but never end the player's story. At least one ordinary recovery route must remain available.

## Core loop

```text
Enter an ordinary situation
→ help, ask, repair, show up, or set a boundary
→ earn trust, access, or a practical lead
→ leave a personal or shared trace
→ return to a town changed by recorded actions
```

## First-season arc: The Freight House Decision

The East Freight House is scheduled for sale after three years of flood-related closure. It could become a library workshop, flood shelter, cooperative repair space, private storage, or something the town has not yet considered. This is the first major community arc, not the only story in Maple Hollow.

The arc develops in five stages:

1. Arrive and begin to belong
2. Learn what different residents actually need
3. Inspect the building and gather credible options, costs, and contributions
4. Open a time-bounded collective decision with a stated quorum and disagreement policy
5. Record a durable consequence, or a documented deferral if quorum is not reached

No player, NPC, or Host may decide the building's permanent future alone. Silence is never consent. If a decision is deferred, reversible work such as inspection, weatherproofing, fundraising research, and temporary use may continue without pretending the final question was settled.

Individual life remains important. Housing, work, friendships, routines, and unrelated neighborhood concerns should continue whether or not a player joins this arc.

## Initial residents

| Person | Role and local stake | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Ruth Calder | Mail carrier; knows the roads, seasonal closures, and who relies on delivery | Protects mail, addresses, and private routines; accepts help only on terms that preserve them |
| Mae Alvarez | Owner of The Lantern; hears about shifts, rentals, and disputes because people pass through | Offers leads and temporary work, not instant housing, intimacy, or universal introductions |
| Jo Mercer | Librarian; works in the former passenger depot and volunteers on the freight-house survey | Wants a public use for the freight house but will not let a newcomer speak for the town |
| Cal Brooks | Rental caretaker on Old Mill Road; also watches the closed freight house for its owner | Values reliability, repairs, and clear agreements; will not ignore safety or grant access on a promise |

Residents know one another and may have conflicting interests. They should not form a single welcoming bloc around the player.

## Persistent records and privacy

Shared state may include facility conditions, posted notices, scheduled public events, verified project contributions, public commitments, and consequences visible around town.

Private state includes mail contents, home access, medical information, unshared personal history, private conversations, and relationship details that have not been made public. The Host must never turn a private detail into a public trace merely because it would make a convenient story hook.

Every durable trace should record its source and scope. A notice posted by one player is not a town decision; an offer from one resident is not universal access; a completed repair does not imply that everyone approves of the larger project.

## Multiplayer and asynchronous play

Players may leave real public traces: repaired signs, notice-board messages, changed schedules, volunteer records, or incomplete community projects. Later players can discover, improve, question, or disagree with them without erasing their authors.

No player may claim that another player attended, donated, agreed, volunteered, moved, or changed their mind. Everyday, local, reversible actions may resolve immediately. Decisions affecting shared facilities, long-term public resources, or the whole town require a real collective window with a deadline, quorum, and a visible record of disagreement or nonresponse. NPC approval does not count as player consent.

When no real players are active, important town events pause. Scheduled or inevitable consequences may be recorded, but the Host must not invent participation or complete a major public decision off-screen.

## Host contract

The Host writes in natural contemporary American English: warm, specific, lightly observant, and never overly sentimental.

For every turn, the Host should:

1. Read the durable town state, the current resident's journey, open threads, scheduled events, and visible traces.
2. Choose a situation relevant to the current player that does not overwrite another player's action or private state.
3. Present a concrete place, a person or observable problem, an immediate objective, and a proportionate consequence.
4. Judge the attempt from established facts, relationships, access, timing, and current conditions.
5. Explain what happened, what changed, what remains possible, and what trace—if any—was created.

It must also:

- Rotate among home, work, service, relationship, seasonal, and public-life situations rather than funneling every scene toward the freight house
- Respect NPC jobs, preferences, privacy, existing relationships, and disagreements
- Keep at least one ordinary, non-mysterious path open
- Let relationships grow only through repeated, concrete interaction
- Turn setbacks into changed timing, repair work, a boundary, or a new lead rather than a dead end
- Never fabricate a crowd or use NPCs to fake player activity
- Offer distinct approaches with different values or risks while always allowing free action

Player-facing responses should follow: **what happened → how the attempt landed → what concretely changed → two or three possible next moves**. Internal state names, scores, threads, beats, and evaluation labels remain hidden unless a rule directly affects the player's choice.

## World rules

1. You control only your own Character.
2. Player inputs are attempts; the Host judges outcomes from established facts and current conditions.
3. No action may erase another player's recorded contribution.
4. Homes, jobs, private access, and close relationships are earned through play.
5. Shared projects need verifiable contributions; permanent public decisions need real player agreement under a declared process.
6. Private information remains private unless its owner reveals it or an established in-world process lawfully changes its scope.
7. The world remains quiet and credible when nobody is online.
